	<title>Vectors</title>

	<introduction>
		<p>
			Up to this point, every variable you have created has stored a single number. That is enough for many problems, but it gets unwieldy fast when you are working with a whole collection of related values — the daily high temperatures for a week, the exam scores for a class, the <m>x</m>-coordinates of points on a curve. This chapter introduces <term>vectors</term>: a way to store an entire ordered collection of numbers under a single variable name, and to operate on the whole collection at once.
		</p>

		<p>
			You have actually been using vectors already, without calling them that. Every time you wrote <pf>1:n</pf> or <pf>[10, 20, 30]</pf> in a for-loop header, you were writing a vector. This chapter promotes those expressions from <q>loop ingredients</q> to first-class data, and shows what you can do with them once they have a name.
		</p>

		<p>
			This chapter covers the following topics:
			<dl>
				<li>
					<title>Creating and Indexing Vectors</title>
					<p>Building vectors with bracket literals, colon notation, and placeholder functions; pulling individual values back out by position; and modifying vectors after they have been created.</p>
				</li>
				<li>
					<title>Vector Arithmetic</title>
					<p>Applying operations and built-in functions to entire vectors at once, without writing a loop. This style is called <term>vectorization</term>, and it is one of the things MATLAB is best known for.</p>
				</li>
				<li>
					<title>Vectors and Loops</title>
					<p>Combining vectors with the for-loops you already know — for the situations where a single vectorized expression is not enough, and for building up a result vector one element at a time.</p>
				</li>
			</dl>
		</p>

		<p>
			By the end of this chapter, you will be able to store and manipulate collections of values, replace many simple loops with single-line vectorized expressions, and write loops that build up a result vector for the cases where vectorization alone is not enough.
		</p>
	</introduction>

	<conclusion>
		<title>🗝️ Key Takeaways...</title>
		<assemblage xml:id="takeaways-vectors">
			<p>
				<dl width="narrow">
					<li>
						<title>Vectors</title>
						<p>An ordered collection of numbers stored under a single variable name.</p>
						<p>Also called one-dimensional arrays, lists, or arrays in other languages.</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Vectors</title>
						<p>
							A vector is an ordered collection of numbers stored under a single name. Other languages call the same idea a <q>list</q> or an <q>array.</q>
						</p>
						<p>
							Row vectors use commas or spaces (<pf>[1, 2, 3]</pf>); column vectors use semicolons (<pf>[1; 2; 3]</pf>); the transpose operator <pf>'</pf> flips between the two.
						</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Building Vectors</title>
						<p>
							<ul marker="square">
								<li>Bracket literals — <pf>[88, 92, 75]</pf> — when you know the values.</li>
								<li>Colon notation — <pf>start:step:stop</pf> — when the values follow a regular pattern.</li>
								<li><pf>zeros(1, n)</pf> and <pf>ones(1, n)</pf> — when you need a placeholder of known size.</li>
							</ul>
						</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Indexing</title>
						<p>
							Use <pf>v(i)</pf> to access the value at position <pf>i</pf>. MATLAB indexes from <pf>1</pf>, not <pf>0</pf>. The keyword <pf>end</pf> always refers to the last position, and you can do arithmetic on it (<pf>v(end - 1)</pf>).
						</p>
						<p>
							An index can itself be a vector: <pf>v(2:4)</pf> and <pf>v([1, 4, 7])</pf> both return slices.
						</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Modifying Vectors</title>
						<p>
							<ul marker="square">
								<li><pf>v(i) = newval</pf> — change a value.</li>
								<li><pf>v(end + 1) = newval</pf> — grow the vector by one.</li>
								<li><pf>v(i) = []</pf> — delete an element; later positions shift down.</li>
							</ul>
						</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Vectorization</title>
						<p>
							Most operators and built-in functions work on whole vectors at once, applying the operation to every element without a loop.
							<ul marker="square">
								<li><pf>+</pf> and <pf>-</pf> work element-wise without a dot.</li>
								<li><pf>.*</pf>, <pf>./</pf>, and <pf>.^</pf> need the dot — plain <pf>*</pf>, <pf>/</pf>, and <pf>^</pf> are reserved for matrix operations.</li>
								<li>Functions like <pf>sqrt</pf>, <pf>sin</pf>, <pf>abs</pf>, and <pf>round</pf> apply to every element automatically.</li>
								<li>Aggregate functions like <pf>sum</pf>, <pf>prod</pf>, <pf>max</pf>, <pf>min</pf>, and <pf>mean</pf> reduce a whole vector to a single number.</li>
							</ul>
						</p>
					</li>
					<li>
						<title>Vectors and Loops</title>
						<p>
							Two ways to loop over a vector:
							<ul marker="square">
								<li><pf>for i = 1:length(v)</pf> — by index, when you need the position.</li>
								<li><pf>for x = v</pf> — by value, when you only need the element.</li>
							</ul>
						</p>
						<p>
							To build a result vector inside a loop, preallocate it with <pf>zeros</pf> first, then fill in <pf>result(i) = ...</pf> one element at a time. Preallocation avoids the cost of resizing the vector on every iteration, and the same advice applies in most other languages.
						</p>
					</li>
				</dl>
			</p>
		</assemblage>
	</conclusion>
